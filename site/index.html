01 / The idea

Not another window.
A change of scenery.

Mountains on the horizon. A long neck in the treetops. Something small stirring by the lake. Omarchasaurus turns a character grid into a place worth pausing for.

Open Enter Immersion from the tray to explore on your focused output. Or choose Start All Outputs for a screensaver-style valley on each display. Both are started manually.

Read the project notes ↗
I.

A little curiosity
goes a long way.

In Enter Immersion, move the mouse over an animal to see its name. Click for a head, jaw or tail reaction, or click an egg to hatch a young Brachiosaurus. Click open sky for a meteor; lake water for a leap and splash. Hatchlings and lake leaps need free animal slots; extra clicks never queue.

II.

Your kind
of quiet.

Morning or Dusk changes the palette, not the world’s behavior. Set reduced motion independently. Tune scenery, optional animals and crowding without tying them to a color scheme.

III.

A new arrangement.
On your terms.

Randomize Valley stages a fresh mix of scenery and optional residents. Review it, then Apply. It never silently rerolls your scene or saves those choices automatically.

02 / Field guideNine kinds of company

Big names.
Bigger discoveries.

Meet the real animals behind the characters. Try saying a name, spot its special shape, then follow a museum link to keep investigating.

A pretend place. Real curiosity.

This valley has a little time-travel magic.

These animals did not all live together: they came from different times and places, millions of years apart. Plesiosaurus belonged in the sea, not our little lake! The colors, friendly reactions and family scenes are artistic choices, not a record of exactly how these animals looked or behaved. The portraits below are original terminal-character art, not fossil photos or scientific reconstructions.

Brachiosaurus Tyrannosaurus rex Triceratops Stegosaurus Ankylosaurus Parasaurolophus Diplodocus Plesiosaurus Pteranodon

Say it in small steps. Hyphens break names into syllables; CAPITAL letters show the stronger beats. Accents and accepted pronunciations vary. These are friendly English readings, not the only right way.

The players say the animal’s name in a generated human voice, not an animal call. There are no real recordings of these extinct animals. Nothing plays automatically. The players work without JavaScript, too. Read how the name audio was made.

Press Play beneath an animal’s name to listen. Pause or replay it right here—you will stay on this page.

01 / Treetop browserDinosaur

Brachiosaurus

Say it BRAK-ee-oh-sore-us

When
Late Jurassic, about 152–145 million years ago
Where
North America (fossils in the USA)
Food
Plants — a herbivore
  • Its front legs were longer than its back legs, giving its body an upward slope.
  • Its long neck helped it reach leaves high in trees.
  • It swallowed leaves without chewing them. Tiny helpers in its gut broke down the tough food.

Tyrannosaurus rex

Say it tie-RAN-oh-sore-us REKS

When
Late Cretaceous, about 68–66 million years ago
Where
North America (Canada and the USA)
Food
Meat — a carnivore
  • Look for a huge head, two strong back legs and very small arms.

  • It hunted animals and also scavenged — eating animals that were already dead.

Stegosaurus

Say it STEG-oh-SORE-us

When
Late Jurassic, about 152–145 million years ago
Where
North America (fossils in the USA)
Food
Plants — a herbivore
  • Its tall back plates stood in two staggered rows, not one straight line.

05 / Built-in armorDinosaur

Ankylosaurus

Say it an-KYE-low-sore-us

When
Late Cretaceous, about 68–66 million years ago
Where
North America (Canada and the USA)
Food
Plants — a herbivore
  • Bony armor covered its wide, low body.

06 / A curious crestDinosaur

Parasaurolophus

Say it pa-ra-saw-ROL-off-us

When
Late Cretaceous, about 77–73 million years ago
Where
North America (Canada and the USA)
Food
Plants — a herbivore
  • Look for the long, backward-pointing crest on this duck-billed dinosaur.
  • Air passages looped inside its hollow crest. Scientists think it may have helped make loud calls.
  • It could move on two or four legs, probably spending much of its time on all fours.

07 / A very long tailDinosaur

Diplodocus

Say it DIP-low-DOCK-us

When
Late Jurassic, about 152–145 million years ago
Where
North America (fossils in the western USA)
Food
Plants — a herbivore
  • A long neck at one end and a whip-like tail at the other made it wonderfully stretched out.
  • Its narrow teeth sat in rows like the teeth of a comb.
  • It was a land animal. Its visits to our lake are part of the valley’s make-believe.

Explore Diplodocus ↗Natural History Museum, London

08 / Ocean swimmerMarine reptile

Plesiosaurus

Say it PLEE-see-uh-SORE-us

When
Early Jurassic, about 200 million years ago
Where
Ancient seas around what is now England
Food
Meat — small prey such as fish and crustaceans
  • It was a marine reptile, not a dinosaur.
  • Four flippers pushed it through the sea. A small head sat at the end of a long neck.
  • Fossil hunter Mary Anning found a nearly complete skeleton in 1823, helping reveal its unusual shape.

Explore Plesiosaurus and its relatives ↗Natural History Museum, London

09 / Above the wavesPterosaur

Pteranodon

Say it ter-AN-oh-don

When
Late Cretaceous, around 85 million years ago
Where
North America, above an inland sea (including today’s Kansas)
Food
Meat — fish
  • It was a pterosaur — a flying reptile, not a dinosaur.
  • Its long beak had no teeth. A dramatic crest stretched behind its head.
  • A very long fourth finger supported each skin-covered wing. These were not feathered bird wings.

Explore Pteranodon longiceps ↗American Museum of Natural History / OLogy

03 / Passing moments

Small moments.
Big personality.

A hatchling at the nest. Rain giving way to a rainbow. A flock crossing the sky. Rare events unfold over active, unpaused session time—and curious visitors can click the nest to start a hatch.

01

Small beginnings

Click an egg or the nest when an animal seat is free. The baby toddles nearby, then scurries off about 30 unpaused seconds after the hatch begins at normal speed; reduced motion takes longer. Once it has fully left, click again. Automatic 8–15-minute hatches remain.

04 / Bring it home

A valley.
One command away.

For Omarchy Quattro, its Quickshell-based shell, and Hyprland. You’ll also need Python 3.11+, Ghostty or Kitty, and a UTF-8 truecolor terminal with a monospace font.

Dependencies must already be installed. No runtime package installer, account, API key or elevated helper.

01

Add the plugin

Your terminal
omarchy plugin add https://github.com/DailenG/Omarchasaurus --enable

Select the command to copy it manually.

02

Find your new tray neighbor

Open the Omarchasaurus tray menu, then choose Enter Immersion. Choose Start All Outputs for screensaver-style sessions; Surface stops only its owned sessions.

05 / Before you wander

A few
field notes.

The complete README ↗
Is this a game I can play in my browser?

No. This website shows static captures from the actual production canvas. The interactive valley runs in a real terminal as an Omarchy Quattro plugin. The Morning / Dusk viewer here demonstrates the two palettes, not live animation.

Can I learn the names without installing anything?

Absolutely. The nine-animal field guide has facts, syllable spellings and museum links. Use the embedded player beneath each name to play, pause or replay its pronunciation without leaving the guide—even without JavaScript. Audio starts only when you choose it. It is generated name speech, not a recording of an animal call, and does not turn on sound in the silent terminal plugin. See the audio sources and creation notes.

How do the animal name labels work?

In the terminal’s Enter Immersion mode, hover your mouse over an animal to reveal its name without clicking. Move away to clear the label. These labels belong to interactive immersion, not screensaver-style Start sessions or this website’s static image. If you use a keyboard or touch, browse the field guide instead.

Will it run on my desktop?

The supported setup is Omarchy Quattro with its Quickshell shell and Hyprland, Python 3.11 or newer, and Ghostty or Kitty. It is not presented as a general Windows, macOS or other Linux desktop app.

Is it an automatic screensaver?

Not yet. In this development preview, you manually choose Enter Immersion or Start All Outputs from the tray. Automatic idle activation and plugin sound are off and not implemented. Ordinary input dismisses screensaver-style Start sessions; immersion is the interactive mode.

Does it connect to anything?

The plugin runtime makes no HTTP/TCP requests. Installation and updates through Omarchy contact GitHub. Runtime integration is local: Hyprland, Omarchy/Quickshell IPC and same-user Unix sockets. This website uses no analytics, cookies, accounts or browser storage. Name audio is served as local website MP3 files, with no external speech service; museum websites load only when you follow their links and have their own policies. Community plugins are unsandboxed user code; this is not a security certification. Read the full security notes.

Can I make it quieter?

Yes. Reduced motion is an independent setting. Choose optional animals, adjust scenery density and set an animal ceiling. Morning and Dusk only change colors. Randomize stages choices for review, rather than saving automatically.

How do I update or remove it?

Surface owned sessions and apply or discard staged settings before updating. Use Omarchy’s normal plugin updater; the README includes the one-time 0.1.0 migration and source-checkout caveats. Removal keeps your saved settings intentionally. Follow the update instructions or removal instructions for the complete steps.
